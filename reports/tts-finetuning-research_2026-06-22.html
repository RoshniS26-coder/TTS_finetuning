2Speaker naming — fresh names vs reusing Sunita/Radha/Isha

Decision — use fresh, distinct names (A/B/C). Deep research on the model card confirms the named speakers are consistency anchors, not specialized voices: the Marathi list is Sanjay, Sunita, Nikhil, Radha, Varun, Isha — and only Sanjay & Sunita are even "recommended." There is no per-name functional specialization (no "news reader" vs "poetry" tuning); pitch/pace/emotion are controlled by the description caption, not baked into the name.

If you fine-tune under Sunita/Radha/Isha

  • Names are learned labels, not protected slots — training overwrites/shifts those pretrained voices toward your recordings.
  • You lose them as baselines/fallbacks, and risk a hybrid identity.
  • Parler maintainer (@ylacombe) notes reused names already don't reproduce reliably even before fine-tuning.
  • No upside — the names give you nothing a fresh name wouldn't.

Benefit of fresh names (A/B/C)

  • Pretrained voices stay intact.
  • Each narrator gets a clean, dedicated slot built entirely from your multi-hour data — more than enough to establish a new name.
  • No collision, no blending.
Critical caveat for your data — one name = one voice The mechanism averages everything under a name into a single voice. So map clean, consistent audio to each name: Panchtantra → name A · v2 (Sunita's clean voice) → name B · v1 → name C. The bedtime-stories set is the problem — 9.5 h of echo + possibly multiple narrators. Labelling it all one name yields a muddy voice. Triage first: delete echoey clips; if there are genuinely 2+ voices, split them into separate names (or set it aside).

Sources: indic-parler-tts model card; Parler-TTS HF discussion (@ylacombe on speaker consistency); Parler-TTS training README; AI4Bharat IndicVoices-R.

3How many epochs

Start at 2–3 epochs with early stopping on eval/loss. 25 h is a large fine-tune set, so it needs far fewer passes than the old 20-min run (which used 6). More data → fewer epochs. Stop at the eval-loss elbow; think in total steps (effective batch × steps), not epochs.
  • Watch for the overfitting signal: train/loss falling while eval/loss rises → stop.
  • Pair the loss curve with logged audio samples each eval — trust your ears over the CE number.
  • Checkpoints every 200 steps + save_total_limit: 3 let you roll back to the best step.

4Is 27.5 h sufficient — or too much?

Sufficient, generously so — and not harmfully "too much." 27.5 h is on par with LJSpeech (~24 h). It is only reasonable because it's spread across ~4–5 narrators + 2 languages (~4–11 h each Marathi, 2.5 h Hindi). For a single clean voice, 5–6 h is plenty and 25 h of one voice would be overkill.
Quality > quantity The echoey / mixed bedtime hours can lower output quality. You will likely get a better model from ~18–20 h of clean, consistent audio than from 27 h that includes muddy clips. Curate before you train.

5Hindi + Marathi in one model — balance

Yes, balance matters — and you're at ~10:1 (25 h Marathi : 2.5 h Hindi). A joint model on that ratio will be strong in Marathi and under-adapted in Hindi.
OptionEffectVerdict
Add Hindi data → ~1:2–1:3 (≈ 8–12 h)Comparable quality both languagesbest for parity
Oversample / upweight the 2.5 h HindiCheap rebalance, risk of Hindi overfitquick fix
Accept Marathi-dominantHindi remains weaker/secondaryif Hindi is low-priority
Separate per-language fine-tunesNo cross-language interference; cleanest qualityif you don't need one model

The base already knows Hindi, so 2.5 h will shift it somewhat — but it won't match the Marathi quality without rebalancing. Keeping the same narrator's voice consistent across languages requires that narrator recorded in both.
